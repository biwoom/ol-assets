## 6.4 The Story about the Elder Mahā Kappina Mahākappinattheravatthu

> **요약**
>
> Dhp 79
> Burlingame: Kappina the Great, Elder
> Compare: Thag-a 235; AN-a 1.14.4.9
> Elder Mahā Kappina, who was formerly a king, was given to exclaiming his delight, and the bhikkhus feared he was recalling the joys he had when king; the Buddha explained the true reason for his exclamations was delight in the Dhamma, and spoke a verse about him.
> Cast: Buddha Padumuttara, Buddha Kassapa, Mahā Kappina, Queen Anojā,
> Keywords: Foremost Disciples, Merit, Devaputtas, Offerings, Past Lives, Renunciation, Previous Buddhas
> ****

“**The one who drinks Dhamma lives well**,”  this Dhamma teaching was given by the Teacher while he was in residence at Jetavana with reference to the Elder Mahā Kappina. The story from beginning to end is as follows:

### 4a Story of the Past: Weavers and Householders

*In times past, they say, venerable Mahā Kappina made his aspiration at the feet of the Buddha Padumuttara, and after passing through successive births and rebirths was at length reborn as the senior weaver in a certain weavers’ village not far from Bārāṇasī. At this time a thousand Paccekabuddhas, who had resided eight months in the Himālaya, were spending the four months of the rainy season in the country; and on a certain occasion they came down into the vicinity of Bārāṇasī and sent eight Paccekabuddhas to the king, requesting manual labour in order to build lodgings.  *[注: AJ:senāsanakaraṇatthāya hatthakammaṁ yācatha; Burlingame had “… sent eight Private Buddhas to the king, asking to be given work in return for their lodging,” which implies, wrongly, the Paccekabuddhas worked for their requisites.]**

*Now just at this time the king was occupied with preparations for the plowing festival. When he heard that Paccekabuddhas had arrived, he came out and enquired their errand. Then he said to them: “Venerable Sirs, I have no time today to attend to your needs, for on the morrow we are to celebrate the plowing festival. But if you will come back again on the third day, I will do as you ask.” And without so much as inviting them to a meal, he turned and reentered his palace. The Paccekabuddhas remarked: “We will go to some other village,” and departed.*

*Just then the wife of the senior weaver, who was on her way to Bārāṇasī on some errand or other, saw the Paccekabuddhas, and saluting them, asked: “Venerable Sirs, how is it that you have come here at such an unsuitable time?” When she had learned all the facts, this woman, richly endowed with faith and intelligence, invited them to a meal, saying: “Venerable Sirs, take your meal with us tomorrow.” – “But there are a great many of us, sister.” – “How many are there of you, venerable Sirs?” – “A thousand.” – “Venerable Sirs, there are just a thousand artisans in this village; each will give food to one  guest; pray accept food from us; I will see to it that you are provided with quarters.”*

*The Paccekabuddhas accepted the invitation, and the woman entered the village and made proclamation: “I saw a thousand Paccekabuddhas and invited them to a meal; arrange seats for these noble persons  and likewise prepare broth, rice, and so forth.” She then had a pavilion erected in the center of the village, caused seats to be arranged, and on the following day provided the Paccekabuddhas with seats and served them with choice food. At the end of the meal, accompanied by all the women in that village, she worshipped the Paccekabuddhas and said: “Venerable Sirs, promise us to remain here during these three months.”*

*Having obtained their promise, she returned to the village and made proclamation once more: “Men and women, let one man from each household among you go to the forest with axes and hatchets, fetch here building materials, and erect quarters for our honored guests.” The villagers obeyed her injunction and built a thousand huts of leaves and grass with night-quarters and day-quarters, each man building one hut. And when the Paccekabuddhas entered upon residence in their respective huts, the villagers offered to minister faithfully to their needs, and faithfully did they minister to them.*

*At the conclusion of the rainy season the woman persuaded each villager to prepare a set of robes for the particular Paccekabuddha who had passed the rainy season in his hut, and saw to it that each of her guests was provided with a set of robes worth a thousand coins. At the conclusion of their residence the Paccekabuddhas returned thanks and departed.*

*Having performed this work of merit, the villagers passed from that state of existence and were reborn as a troop of Devaputtas in the Realm of the Thirty-Three. After enjoying celestial glory in that state of existence,  they were reborn in the dispensation of the Buddha Kassapa as householders of Bārāṇasī. The senior weaver was the son of the senior householder, and the wife of the senior weaver was the daughter of the senior householder. All of those women also, on reaching marriageable age, married their former husbands.*

*Now one day those householders heard the announcement that the Teacher was to teach the Dhamma at the monastery; therefore all of them, accompanied by their wives, went to the monastery to hear the Dhamma. Scarcely had they entered the enclosure of the monastery when it began to rain. Persons who had intimate friends or kinsmen  among the novices or bhikkhus found shelter in their cells; but the company of householders, having no friends or relatives at the monastery, were unable to gain entrance and were obliged to remain unprotected in the monastery enclosure.*

*The senior householder said to them: “See what a plight we are in; respectable persons ought to be ashamed to be in such a plight.” – “But, sir, what are we to do?” – “We have fallen into this plight because we are not on terms of intimacy with the bhikkhus; let us contribute money and build a monastery.” – “Very well, sir.” The senior householder gave a thousand coins, the other householders five hundred apiece, and each of the women two hundred and fifty.*

*Having collected the money,  they began the erection of what is called a Great Hermitage, crowned with a thousand pinnacles, to serve as a place of residence for the Teacher; and when, by reason of the great extent of the work they had undertaken, the money proved to be insufficient, each of those who had contributed before gave half as much again. When the hermitage was completed, they held a festival in honor of the opening of the monastery and for seven days gave rich gifts to the Saṅgha of bhikkhus presided over by the Buddha, presenting each of the twenty thousand bhikkhus with a set of robes.*

*But the wife of the senior householder, although she had already done the same as the rest had done, determined in her wisdom to do yet more. Said she: “I will do honor to the Teacher.” Accordingly she took a garment of the color of anojā flowers,  *[注: AJ: it is an orange colour.]* worth a thousand coins, and a casket of anojā flowers, and when it was time for the Teacher to return thanks, she honored him with a present of the anojā flowers; and then casting the garment at his feet, made this aspiration: “Venerable Sir, in my future states of existence may my body be of the hue of the anojā flower and may my name be ‘Anojā.’” – “So be it,” replied the Teacher, returning thanks. Having lived out their allotted term of life, all of them passed from that state of existence and were reborn in the Deva Realm.*

### 4b Story of the Present: King Kappina and Queen Anojā

Passing from the Deva Realm, the senior householder was reborn in the royal household of the city Kukkuṭavatī. King Mahā Kappina was his name. The rest of the company were reborn in the households of courtiers. The wife of the senior householder was reborn  in the royal household of the kingdom of Maddā in the city of Sāgala. Her body was of the hue of the anojā flower, and “Anojā” was the name they gave her.  When she reached marriageable age she was married to King Kappina and became known as Queen Anojā. The rest of the women were reborn in the households of courtiers, and when they reached marriageable age were married to the sons of those same courtiers.

All of them enjoyed glory like the glory of the king. Whenever the king rode in procession, mounted on his elephant and adorned with all his adornments, they also rode in procession in like state; whenever the king went about on his horse or in his chariot, they also went about in like manner. Thus it was that since as one company they had performed works of merit, as one company also they enjoyed equal glory.

Now the king had five horses, Vāla, Puppha, Vāḷavāhana, Pupphavāhana, and Supatta. One of these horses, Supatta, he alone rode; the other four he allowed riders to use for carrying messages. Early one morning after breakfast he sent out the four riders with this command: “Ride forth and scour the country for two or three leagues about and if you learn of the appearance of the Buddha or the Dhamma or the Saṅgha, come back and bring me the good news.” The riders rode forth from the four gates and scoured the country for two or three leagues about, but returned with no news.

One day the king mounted his horse and accompanied by a retinue of courtiers, proceeded to his pleasure-garden. Seeing five hundred weary-looking traders entering the city, he said: “These men are weary from a journey; perhaps we shall hear some good news from them.”  So he summoned them and asked them: “Whence do you come?” – “Your majesty, there is a city called Sāvatthī 120 leagues from here; thence do we come.” – “Is there any news from your country?” – “None other than this, your majesty, that the Sambuddha has appeared.”

Straightaway the king’s whole body was thrilled with the five sorts of joy; for a moment he hesitated, for he was unable to collect his thoughts; then he said: “Friends, what is it that you say?” – “The Buddha has appeared, your majesty.” Twice and thrice did the king hesitate and speak as before. And again a fourth time he said: “Friends, what is it that you say?” – “The Buddha has appeared, your majesty.” – “Friends, I give you 100,000 coins; is there any other news besides?” – “Yes, your majesty, there is; the Dhamma has appeared.”

When he heard this also, the king hesitated and spoke three times as before, and when for the fourth time he heard the word “Dhamma,” he said: “Here, I give you 100,000 coins.”

Then he asked them: “Friends, is there any other news besides?” – “Yes, your majesty, there is; the Saṅgha has appeared.” When the king heard this also, he hesitated and spoke three times as before, and when for the fourth time he heard the word “Saṅgha,” he said: “Here, once more do I give you 100,000 coins.”

Having so done, he surveyed his thousand courtiers and asked them: “Friends, what is your pleasure?” – “Your majesty, what is your pleasure?” – “Friends, I have heard the good news: ‘The Buddha has appeared, the Dhamma has appeared, the Saṅgha has appeared,’ therefore I shall not return to my palace again, but for the sake of the Teacher I shall go forth under him.” – “Your majesty, we too will go forth with you.”

The king caused a message to be written on a plate of gold and said to the merchants:  “Queen Anojā will give you 300,000 coins so soon as you give her this message: ‘The King’s dominion is given into your hands; enjoy the glory thereof at your good pleasure.’” And he added: “Should she ask you, however, where the king is, tell her that for the sake of the Teacher he has departed to go forth under him.” The king’s courtiers also sent similar messages to their wives. And as soon as the king had dismissed the traders, he departed with his retinue of a thousand courtiers.

Early in the morning of that day the Teacher surveyed the world, and seeing King Mahā Kappina with his retinue, became aware of the following: “Yonder Mahā Kappina has heard from the traders of the appearance of the Three Jewels, has rewarded them with 300,000 coins for bringing him word, has renounced his kingdom, and purposes on the morrow, accompanied by his retinue of a thousand courtiers, to go forth for my sake and go forth; he and his retinue will attain Arahatship together with the analytic knowledges; it is necessary for me to go to meet him.” Accordingly on the following day, like a Universal Monarch going forth to meet the headman of a little village, he took bowl and robe and went forth, and having traveled two hundred leagues he sat down on the bank of the river Candabhāgā under a banyan tree, and there he remained, diffusing rays of six colors.

As the king proceeded on his way he came to a certain river.  “What river is this?” he asked. “The river Aravacchā, your majesty.” – “How deep is it and how wide is it, friends?”  – “It is one league deep and two leagues wide, your majesty.” – “Is there a boat here, or a raft?” – “There is not, your majesty.” – “While we are looking for boats and rafts, birth is bringing us to old age and old age is bringing us to death. Free from doubt, I have renounced the world for the sake of the Three Jewels; by their supernatural power may this water be to me unlike water.” Having thus considered the virtues of the Three Jewels, the king meditated upon the Buddha, saying: “Such is he, the Fortunate One, the Worthy One, the Perfect Sambuddha, the one endowed with understanding and good conduct.” While thus engaged in meditation the king and his retinue dashed over the surface of the river on their thousand horses, the Sindh horses springing upon the surface of the river as on a flat rock, without so much as wetting the tips of their hoofs.

Having crossed the river Aravacchā, the king proceeded until he came to yet another river. “What is the name of this river?” he asked. “The river Nīlavāhanā, your majesty.” – “How deep is it and how wide is it?” – “Half a league deep and half a league wide, your majesty.” The rest exactly as before, except that when the king saw this river he said: “The Dhamma has been well-proclaimed by the Fortunate One,” and crossed by meditating on the Dhamma.

Having crossed the river Nīlavāhanā, the king proceeded until he came to yet a third river. “What is the name of this river?” he asked. “The river Candabhāgā, your majesty.” – “How deep is it and how wide is it?” – “A league deep and a league wide, your majesty.” The rest exactly as before, except that when the king saw this river he said:  “The Fortunate One’s Saṅgha of disciples are good in their practice,” and crossed by meditating on the Saṅgha.

After crossing the third river as the king continued his journey, he saw the rays of light of six colors which issued from the body of the Teacher; the branches and forks and leaves of the banyan tree appeared as though made of pure gold. The king thought to himself: “This radiance is not that of the moon or sun, nor yet that of any mighty Nāga or Garuḍa; it must be that, setting out as I have for the sake of the Teacher, I have been seen by the great Gotama Buddha.”

Accordingly he dismounted at once from his horse and inclining his body to the direction of the rays, approached the Teacher; and penetrating the circle of the Buddha’s rays as one might plunge into a sea of vermilion, he worshipped the Teacher and with his  retinue of a thousand courtiers seated himself respectfully on one side.

The Teacher taught the Dhamma in orderly sequence, and at the conclusion of his discourse the king and his company of courtiers were established in the fruition of Stream-entry, whereupon all of them arose with one accord and requested to go forth.

The Teacher considered within himself: “Will these noblemen receive bowls and robes created by psychic power?” and became aware of the following: “These noblemen gave a thousand robes to a thousand Paccekabuddhas, and in the dispensation of the Buddha Kassapa also gave twenty thousand robes to twenty thousand bhikkhus; it is not wonderful that they should receive bowls and robes created by psychic power.”

Therefore he extended his right hand and said:  “Come, bhikkhus, the Dhamma has been well-proclaimed, ive the spiritual life for the complete ending of suffering.” Straightaway they were provided with the eight monastic requisites, becoming as it were elders of a hundred years standing, and first soaring into the air, they returned to earth, worshipped the Teacher, and sat down.

The traders went to the royal palace, announced that they had been sent by the king, and upon being invited to enter by the queen, entered, made obeisance, and stood respectfully on one side. The queen asked them: “Sirs, on what errand have you come?” – “Your majesty, we were sent to you by the king, who gave us 300,000 coins.” – “Sirs, it is a large sum of money you mention; what did you do for the king that pleased him so greatly that he gave you that amount of money?” – “Nothing much, your majesty; all we did was to bring the king a certain piece of news.” – “Are you permitted to tell me also what it was?” – “Yes, your majesty.” – “Well then, sirs, tell me.” – “Your majesty: ‘The Buddha has appeared in the world.’”

When the queen heard this, she was affected precisely as the king had been; her body was suffused with joy, and three times she failed to grasp the meaning of what she heard. When she heard the word “Buddha” the fourth time, she enquired: “What did the king give you when he heard this word?” – “100,000 coins, your majesty.” – “Sirs, the king did not reward you suitably when he gave you only 100,000 coins for bringing him such a message; it is a poor present I give you in presenting you with 300,000 coins. Did you bring the king any other message?”  – “Such and such,” said they, repeating the two other messages. As before, the queen’s  body was suffused with joy at each of the messages she heard; three times she failed to grasp the meaning of what she heard, but the fourth time she heard each message she presented them with 300,000 coins. Thus in all they received 1,200,000 coins.

Then the queen asked them: “Sirs, where is the king?” – “Your majesty, he has departed, saying: ‘For the sake of the Teacher I will go forth.’” – “Did he send me any message?” – “All his kingly power is given into your hands; enjoy the glory thereof at your own good pleasure.” – “And where are his courtiers, sirs?” – “Your majesty, they also went away, saying: ‘We will go forth with the king.’” Thereupon the queen summoned the wives of the courtiers and said to them: “Friends, your husbands have departed, saying: ‘We will go forth with the king,’ what will you do?” – “But what message did they send to us, your majesty?” – “They have given the glory they possess into your hands to enjoy according to your own good pleasure.” – “But, your majesty, what do you intend to do?”

“Friends, he who but now was king made ready for the journey, and having honored the Three Jewels with 300,000 coins and having cast off his glory as he would eject a mass of saliva, departed to go forth. I also have learned of the appearance of the Three Jewels and have honored the Three Jewels with hundreds of thousands more. The glory which spells suffering to the king spells suffering to me also. Who would get down on his knees and take into his mouth a mass of saliva spat out by the king? I have no need of real glory; I also will go forth for the sake of the Teacher.” – “Your majesty, then we also will go forth with you.” – “Well and good, friends, if you are able.” – “We are able, your majesty.”  “Well then, come,” said the queen.

So she caused a thousand chariots to be harnessed, mounted her chariot and departed, accompanied by her retinue. Coming to the first river on the journey, she asked the same questions the king had asked and received the same answers, whereupon she said to her companions: “Look for the way taken by the king.” They replied: “Your majesty, we see no footprints of Sindh horses.” The queen said: “The king must have pronounced an Act of Truth,  *[注: BG: For a discussion of this charm, see my paper, The Act of Truth (Saccakiriyā); a Hindu Spell and its Employment as a Psychic Motif in Hindu Fiction,JRAS., July, 1917. For other occurrences of the charm, see stories 1.3a, 13.6 and 17.3b.]* saying: ‘For the sake of the Three  Jewels I have renounced the world and so crossed. I also have renounced the world for the sake of the Three Jewels; by their supernatural power may this water be to me unlike water.” And meditating thus on the power of the Three Jewels, she ordered her thousand chariots to go forward. The water was like a flat rock, insomuch that not even the outer rims of the wheels were wetted. In like manner also she crossed the remaining two rivers.

When the Teacher became aware of her approach, he rendered the bhikkhus invisible that they might not be seen sitting with him. As she drew nearer and nearer and saw the rays of light issuing from the body of the Teacher, the same thought came to her as had previously come to the king. Having approached the Teacher, she worshipped him, stood respectfully on one side, and asked him: “Venerable Sir, I think Mahā Kappina has come to you and told you that he has renounced the world for your sake. Where is he? Show him to us.” – “Just sit down; you will presently see him even here.”  The hearts of all those women were filled with joy at the thought that while seated even there they should see their husbands. So they sat down.

The Teacher taught the Dhamma in orderly sequence. At the conclusion of his discourse the queen and her retinue were established in the fruition of Stream-entry. The Elder Mahā Kappina and his retinue, who heard the Teacher teach the Dhamma to the women, attained Arahatship together with the analytic knowledges. At that moment the Teacher showed the bhikkhus to the women.

We are told that the reason why the Teacher did not show the bhikkhus to the women at the very moment when they arrived, was for fear that should they see their husbands with yellow robes and shaven heads, their minds would be disturbed and they would therefore be unable to attain the path and the fruitions. Hence it was that he waited until the women were firmly grounded in faith to show them the bhikkhus in their state as Arahats.

When the women saw the bhikkhus, they worshipped them with the five limbs, and said: “Venerable Sirs, now have you reached the goal of your spiritual life.” Having so said, they worshipped the Teacher, stood respectfully on one side, and requested to go forth. We are told that when they made this request, some of the bhikkhus said: “The Teacher thought of the coming of Uppalavaṇṇā.” But the Teacher said to those female lay disciples: “Go to Sāvatthī and enter the spiritual life in the monastery of bhikkhunīs.”

So those female lay disciples started out on foot and journeyed from place to place, the populace everywhere offering them hospitality and bestowing honor upon them, and after a journey of 120 leagues they reached the monastery of bhikkhunīs, went forth, and attained Arahatship. The Teacher taking the thousand bhikkhus with him, flew through the air to Jetavana.

At Jetavana venerable Mahā Kappina went about the night-quarters and the day-quarters  breathing forth the solemn utterance: “Oh happiness! Oh happiness!” The bhikkhus reported the matter to the Fortunate One, saying: “Venerable Sir, venerable Mahā Kappina is going about saying: ‘Oh happiness! Oh happiness!’ Presumably he is talking about the happiness of his own rule as king.”

The Teacher sent for him and said to him: “Kappina, is it true, as they say, that you are uttering exalted utterances regarding the happiness of love and the happiness of rule?” – “Venerable Sir, the Fortunate One himself knows whether or not I am uttering exalted utterances regarding happiness of that kind.”

The Teacher said to the bhikkhus: “Bhikkhus, it is not with reference to the happiness of ruling that my son is uttering exalted utterances. He that drinks the Dhamma delights in the Dhamma. It is with reference to the Deathless Nibbāna that he is breathing forth these solemn utterances of joy.” And having so spoken, the Teacher joined the connection and instructed them in the Dhamma by pronouncing the following verse:

> **79. Dhammapīti sukhaṁ seti, vippasannena cetasā,
> Ariyappavedite Dhamme sadā ramati paṇḍito.**

> The one who drinks Dhamma lives well,
> with a clear mind, the Dhamma that
> is made known by the Noble Ones
> the wise one always delights in.

At the end of the teaching many became Stream-enterers and so on.
